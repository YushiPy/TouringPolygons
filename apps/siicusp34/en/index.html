What the algorithm does: once you choose the order and, in the next challenges, the convex pieces, our implementation computes where the path must touch each region to be as short as possible.

The Touring Polygons Problem (TPP) asks for the shortest path that starts at a point s, visits a set of polygons and ends at a point t; touching the boundary or crossing the interior of a polygon counts as a visit. In the free-order variant the visiting order is also unknown. When the route is a closed tour, this is the Traveling Salesman Problem with Neighborhoods (TSPN): it generalizes the TSP from points to polygonal regions, whose boundaries can approximate any simple closed curve arbitrarily well. Below we show how our solver finds a shortest tour.

How the algorithm finds the shortest tour

Six cards show the essential steps of the algorithm; the glossary below explains the more specific terms.

  1. 01 · UPPER BOUND

    Find a feasible route

    A greedy heuristic finds a feasible route and sets the incumbent U. In challenge 1 it misses the optimum: the dashed route is shorter and only appears after the search.

  2. 02 · SEARCH NODE

    Keep partial orders

    With only A and B fixed in this order, the shortest route also touches C by chance, but misses D and E. Only A and B belong to this node's sequence.

  3. 03 · LOWER BOUND

    Evaluate the node and prune

    Each node is a fixed-order route through convex polygons. With fixed endpoints, Dror et al. solve it in polynomial time; we designed an exact algorithm for the closed tour. Omitting targets cannot lengthen a route, so its length is a proved lower bound L for the whole branch; if L reaches U, within the tolerance, the branch is pruned.

  4. 04 · FEASIBLE ROUTE

    Update the incumbent

    With every polygon of the instance included, the final route visits all five. When a feasible route is shorter than the current incumbent, we update U.

  5. 05 · INSERTION

    Generate the m + 1 insertions

    We pick the unvisited polygon farthest from the route. With m polygons in the sequence, inserting P gives m + 1 positions: before, between or after the ones already ordered.

  6. 06 · REFINEMENT

    Refine into convex pieces

    A non-convex region starts as its convex hull. When a route crosses the hull without visiting the region, the search branches on its convex pieces. In challenge 2, the three polygons are split into pieces, and the path shown touches one piece of each.

Key terms
Incumbent and upper bound U
The best feasible route found so far is the incumbent; its length is U. Since this route exists, the optimum cannot be longer than U.
Partial order
A sequence that fixes the order of only some polygons. The missing ones do not constrain the node's subproblem.
Convex relaxation
At each node, the order of the selected targets is fixed, each target is represented by its convex hull before refinement (or by one convex piece after it), and the polygons not yet in the sequence are left out. Our oracle solves this subproblem with a floating-point stage whose bounds are proved with directed rounding; when they are not tight enough, an exact algorithm in rational arithmetic returns the optimal tour with zero error.
Lower bound L
A bound valid for every extension of the node, given by the proved bound of the convex oracle. It is not necessarily the length of a feasible route; the comparison with U takes the tolerance into account.
Branch and bound
Branching creates children for alternatives that cover the remaining possibilities. Pruning discards a branch when its lower bound cannot improve the incumbent. The search combines these bounds with the exploration of the tree.

2003DEFINITION AND FIRST ALGORITHM

Dror et al. introduce the TPP

Dror, Efrat, Lubiw and Mitchell introduce the Touring Polygons Problem and describe an algorithm for disjoint convex polygons visited in a fixed order, with fixed start and end points. For n vertices in total and k polygons, the paper gives time O(kn log(n/k)). The distinction matters: the paper describes an algorithm; we do not attribute an implementation to it.

[1] Touring a sequence of polygons
2017LOWER COMPLEXITY

Tan & Jiang lower the asymptotic complexity

Revisiting the problem, Tan and Jiang describe a simpler way to build the shortest-path maps. For disjoint convex polygons they reach O(kn); for polygons that may intersect, O(k²n). The asymptotic gain comes with a larger overhead, which made the algorithm slower on the instances we tested.

2026STATE OF THE ART

Fekete et al. solve the TSPN

The Algorithms Division of TU Braunschweig, led by Sándor P. Fekete, presents a branch-and-bound algorithm for the TSPN: given polygonal regions, possibly non-convex, find the shortest closed tour that visits them all and returns to where it started; that point is not fixed. The algorithm also searches the order and solves each convex subproblem as a second-order cone program (SOCP) with Gurobi. Its 558 benchmark instances are the ones we compare on.

[3] A Branch-and-Bound Algorithm for the TSPN

WHERE WE COME IN

We started this project in July 2025 by implementing the algorithm described by Dror et al. and presented that first version at SIICUSP33. By early 2026 we had also implemented the algorithm of Tan and Jiang. Its asymptotic gain was elegant, but the overhead made it inefficient on the more realistic instances we wanted to study.

We then turned to the non-convex fixed-order TPP, aiming to turn the implementation into a paper. As far as we knew, we were the only group with an implemented algorithm for that variant. The direction changed in March, during a conversation with Prahlad Narasimhan Kasthurirangan about his work on convex covers — collections of convex polygons whose union is the original polygon — [5]. He pointed us to Michael Perk, then in Fekete's group.

That is how we found the group's new work on the TSPN. The race seemed lost, but a closer reading revealed an opportunity: although the paper cited Dror et al., its convex subproblems were formulated as second-order cone programs (SOCPs) and solved with Gurobi. We already had a dedicated implementation of that subproblem and asked whether it could be a more efficient alternative.

We spent the following months refining that specialization:

  • Exactness: we designed an exact algorithm for the closed tour through convex polygons. Given rational input, it returns the optimal tour with zero error; an interior-point SOCP solver only approaches the optimum within a tolerance. Our tours stay within 3 × 10−11 of every polygon.
  • Speed: both searches explore about as many nodes, but a node of Fekete et al., with the SOCP solver, costs about 238 times as much as one of ours. On the 541 instances both solve with one thread, ours is faster on every one: median speedup 73×, arithmetic mean 196×.
  • Search: we refined the branch and bound and kept lazy evaluation: a child is evaluated only when it leaves the queue.
  • Autonomy: our convex solver removes the need for Gurobi, and our implementation of Greene's algorithm removes the need for CGAL — both tools with restricted commercial use.

In this benchmark, the combination was faster, more accurate and more robust on this set of instances. The comparison should be read within that experimental scope — not as a universal promise for every TSPN variant.

These results are on the 558 instances of Fekete et al. [3], with 4 to 60 polygons, as closed tours with free order. We ran their original, unmodified code and our solver on the same AMD Ryzen 7 9700X. Each configuration had three 300 s runs, plus one 900 s run on the instances left open; solved means a relative gap of 10−3, as in [3].

Where does the gain come from?

On the 166 instances where Fekete et al. take at least 1 s with one thread, our solver is 257× faster. Both searches explore about as many nodes (theirs explores 1.08 times as many), but each of their nodes, with the SOCP solver, costs 238× as much as one of ours. All three are geometric means: 1.08 × 238 ≈ 257.
