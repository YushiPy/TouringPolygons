\section{Objectives}

The Touring Polygons Problem (TPP) asks for a shortest Euclidean path that starts at a point \(s\), visits a sequence of polygons \(P_1,\ldots,P_k\) in the plane, and ends at a point \(t\). Touching the boundary or crossing the interior of a polygon counts as a visit. In the free-order variant the visiting order is also unknown; when the route is a closed tour, this is the Traveling Salesman Problem with Neighborhoods (TSPN) [3]. Targets that are areas rather than points arise in last-mile delivery, surveillance, and data collection in precision agriculture, and real regions are often non-convex.

Both variants are NP-hard for non-convex polygons, but the fixed-order TPP with convex polygons is solvable in polynomial time [1, 2]. This work aims to exploit that gap: to build an exact, self-contained solver for the free-order problem whose core is an exact geometric algorithm for the convex case, with no commercial optimizer, and to compare it with the state of the art, the branch-and-bound algorithm of Fekete et al. [4], which solves the convex subproblems as second-order cone programs (SOCP) with Gurobi.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\textbf{Convex subproblem.} For pairwise disjoint convex polygons, Dror et al. [1] build last-step shortest-path maps in \(O(nk\log(n/k))\) time, where \(n\) is the total number of vertices; Tan and Jiang [2] improve the bound to \(O(nk)\). We implemented both and extended the map-based algorithm with memoization, so that only the map regions actually queried are built, and with closed tours. Each call returns a feasible route and a lower bound proved with interval arithmetic, falling back to an interior-point polish or an exact rational computation when the interval proof is not tight enough.

\textbf{Branch and bound.} A node holds a partial sequence of targets, each represented by its convex hull or by one convex piece of an optimal convex decomposition, computed by our own implementation of Greene's algorithm [5]. Solving the convex TPP on this sequence gives a lower bound \(L\) for every extension of the node, since omitting targets cannot lengthen a route. If the route already visits every original polygon, it is feasible and may improve the incumbent \(U\). Otherwise a missing polygon is inserted in each of the \(m+1\) positions of the sequence, or a hull that the route crosses without visiting its polygon is replaced, in separate children, by each of its convex pieces. Nodes with \(L\ge U\) are pruned. The insertion and refinement rules follow [4]; the search uses best-first order with periodic depth-first dives and an initial heuristic tour.

\textbf{Benchmark.} We used the 558 instances of [4] (4 to 60 polygons from city maps, random polygons, and tessellations) and ran the original, unmodified code of [4] in the paper's configuration (16 threads) and with one thread, on the same machine as our solver (AMD Ryzen 7 9700X, Linux). Each run had 300 s, repeated three times, and unsolved instances got one more 900 s run. An instance counts as solved at a relative gap of \(10^{-3}\), the criterion of [4].

\section{Results}

Our run of the paper's configuration (16 threads) reproduces the published results exactly: 542 of 558 instances (97.13\%) in 300 s and 549 (98.39\%) in 900 s. With one thread, [4] solves 523 in 300 s and 541 in 900 s. Our solver, also with one thread, solves 557 in 300 s; the last one, open for every configuration of [4], closes in 89 minutes. No instance is solved only by [4], and ours is faster on each of the 541 instances that both solve with one thread (Figure 2), from 1.9 to 5{,}244 times. Summed over these instances, the time is 15{,}767 s for [4] and 34 s for ours.

Where [4] takes at least 1 s with one thread, the two search trees have about the same size (1.08 nodes of [4] per node of ours); the gain comes from the cost of each node, 238 times smaller with our geometric oracle than with the SOCP solver.

Geometric accuracy differs as well. The tours of our solver stay within \(3\times10^{-11}\) of every polygon, whereas those of [4], within its feasibility tolerance, miss polygons by a median of \(2.7\times10^{-6}\) and up to \(9.6\times10^{-4}\) in instance units; only 151 of its 558 tours pass validation at \(10^{-7}\). On a real instance with 96 non-convex districts of São Paulo [6], our solver proved, in 49 minutes with 6 threads, that the published tour is optimal within a relative gap of \(10^{-6}\).

\section{Conclusions}

Embedding an exact geometric algorithm for the convex TPP in a branch-and-bound tree gives an exact solver for the TSPN with polygonal neighborhoods that is one to two orders of magnitude faster than the state of the art, closes every benchmark instance, and returns tours that visit every region up to rounding error, without a commercial optimizer. Optimality is certified up to the stated relative gaps, with lower bounds proved in interval or exact arithmetic. Future work includes larger instances, routes with several vehicles, and the vehicle routing problem with polygonal neighborhoods.
